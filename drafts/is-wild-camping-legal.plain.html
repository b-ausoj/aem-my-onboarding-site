Is wild camping legal in Switzerland?

There is no single national wild-camping law in Switzerland. As a rule of thumb: a single-night bivouac high in the mountains, above the treeline, is broadly tolerated. Pitching a tent in valleys, forests, farmland or on private land generally is not. And it is forbidden outright in the Swiss National Park, nature reserves and wildlife rest zones.

Always check the local and cantonal rules before you go — this is guidance, not legal advice.

That one answer hides a lot of nuance. Here is what it actually depends on, and how to stay on the right side of the line.

It’s a bivouac, not “camping”

Swiss tolerance rests on a distinction. A bivouac is a single, minimal overnight — arrive at dusk, leave at dawn, a small shelter, no trace. Camping means pitching a tent for longer, lower down, in valleys or organized settings. The mountains tolerate the former far more than the latter, and much of the law and etiquette follows from it.

Read more: Bivouac vs. camping — the difference that matters.

The treeline rule of thumb

Above the treeline — the high, open terrain of rock, scree and alpine meadow — a discreet one-night bivouac is generally accepted across most of the Alps. Below it, in forests and cultivated valleys, you are on land that is owned, farmed or protected, and wild camping is generally not permitted without the owner’s consent.

Always forbidden

Some places are a hard “no”, wherever you are in the country:

  • The Swiss National Park — all camping and bivouacking is forbidden.
  • Nature reserves and protected biotopes — habitats protected by law.
  • Wildlife rest zones and game reserves (Wildruhezonen, Jagdbanngebiete) — especially sensitive in winter.

It varies by canton

Beyond these national rules, the specifics are cantonal — and some regions, such as the Alpstein/Appenzell area, have added restrictions and designated spots in response to overtourism. Check your destination before you rely on the general rule.

How to do it right

If you do bivouac above the treeline: choose durable ground away from water, trails and livestock; keep your group small; arrive late and leave early; use a stove, never an open fire; and pack out everything you bring in.
